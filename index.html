Serving people, one patient at a time.

For more than fifteen years I’ve worked on national-security problems: first through independent research on pharmaceutical supply chains, counter-narcotics and illicit finance, then as an engineer at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory and the U.S. Department of State. That work taught me to feel responsible for people I’d never meet: individuals, communities and organizations, at home and around the world. When I left national security, I chose medicine because it asks for the same responsibility, but one patient at a time.

Today I work full time in patient care at the University of Washington, both alongside doctors and nurses and on my own, and I’m completing my pre-medical coursework. I’ve been inspired by the physicians and nurses I’ve worked beside, and by the ones who have cared for me as a patient. This site is where I learn in the open, and where I try to give something back.

Where I am now

At the bedside.

Since 2025 I’ve worked full time at the University of Washington as a Medical Assistant–Phlebotomist and Clinical Laboratory Technician. I’m often the first person a patient sees in the morning, and the needle is rarely anyone’s favorite part of the day. I’ve learned to:

  • slow down;
  • say what I’m doing and why;
  • get every label right, because the whole care team relies on it.

Learning from physicians.

I’ve spent about 300 hours shadowing in:

  • critical care, including the ICU and the cardiothoracic ICU;
  • anesthesiology;
  • geriatrics, and palliative, comfort and end-of-life care;
  • oncology rounds.

I went to learn medicine. I also learned how much of it is listening.

Giving back.

For about 300 hours since 2023, I’ve:

  • taught English and helped immigrant families settle into a new life;
  • cared for animals at the Humane Society.

What I’ve built, and why

An MCAT study app, for anyone coming to medicine from somewhere else

I didn’t come from a medical background. I’m studying for the MCAT while I make the transition, working full time in a hospital. Most prep is written for students who have spent years in science classes and have hours free every day. I built the tool I needed instead.

It teaches the method behind each kind of question, step by step, before it ever tests you. It brings back your weakest topics first, and it keeps each day to a plan a working adult can finish.

It’s free and open to anyone, with no account needed. If you’re changing careers, working while you study, or the first in your family to try for medicine, I built it for you too.

Why this site exists

I believe good medicine should reach everyone who needs it. As a student, my part is small. I study the evidence carefully, share what I learn openly, and build free tools that make preparing for medicine more accessible. My interests are anesthesiology, critical care and cancer care, and I’m especially drawn to how technology can make care safer and easier to reach. I hope to keep contributing to that work as I train.

What I hold myself to

  • Honesty about what I know. I’m a student. These are learning projects, not medical advice, and I say so plainly.
  • Show my sources. If I make a clinical claim, you can see where it comes from.
  • Own my mistakes. When I find an error, I correct it and keep a record. Medicine depends on people who do that.
  • Remember who it’s for. Every chart, lab value and study is about a person and a family.

I use AI tools to help me draft and check my writing. The questions, the reading and the judgments are mine, and so is the responsibility for getting them right.
