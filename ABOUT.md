# About me

I’m a pre-medical student working full time in patient care at the University of Washington. I came to medicine after more than fifteen years in security and national-security work, and I’m now completing my science prerequisites.

## Why medicine

For more than fifteen years I’ve worked on national-security problems: first through independent research on pharmaceutical supply chains, counter-narcotics and illicit finance, then as an engineer at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory and the U.S. Department of State. That work carries a quiet kind of responsibility. You’re accountable to people you will never meet: individuals, communities, businesses and organizations, here at home and around the world. Whether the threat is to someone’s safety, health or security, the job is to protect them and to be honest when something goes wrong.

When I left national security, I chose medicine because it’s the same calling, practiced one patient at a time. I’ve seen it from both sides of the bed. As a patient, I’ve been cared for by physicians and nurses whose skill and kindness stayed with me. At the University of Washington I now work with patients every day, both alongside doctors and nurses and on my own. I’ve learned that the small, exact parts of care matter: confirming who someone is, labeling correctly and explaining what I’m doing. So does a few calm minutes with someone who is frightened.

What I love most about medicine is that everyone who comes through the door deserves the same dignity and the same care, whatever their background, faith, income, race or gender. A good clinician takes the time to understand each person’s life and culture, so the care fits them. But the starting point is always the same: a person who needs help.

I want to become a physician who brings both of my careers together. From national security, I bring an engineer’s discipline about safety and a habit of accountability. From patient care, I bring respect for the person in the bed and the people who love them. I hope to practice in Washington, including in communities that face barriers to care.

## Why I share what I build

I didn’t come from a medical background, and I know how hard the transition can be. So I built a [free MCAT study app](https://mcat.collinbgeorge.com/) for people like me: career-changers, working adults and anyone without a head start in science. It teaches the method behind each question before it tests you. I share it, and the rest of this site, openly. I believe the path into medicine, and good information about medicine, should be available to anyone who wants it.

I’m a student, and I try to be honest about what that means:
